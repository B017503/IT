LESSON 2.1

Internal Threats

Threats that originate from people already inside an organisation — employees, contractors, or third-party vendors — which is exactly why they're often harder to spot: the person already has legitimate access.

What makes a threat "internal"?

  • Comes from someone within the organisation — staff, contractors, vendors.
  • Can be accidental (human error, negligence) or deliberate (malicious intent).
  • Harder to detect than external attacks because the perpetrator already has system or data access.

Employee sabotage

Theft or loss — physical equipment (laptops, USB drives, phones), data (client lists, financial info), or software/licences (copying proprietary code).

Unauthorised software — legal risk from breaching licences, compatibility problems, malicious uses (keyloggers, spy tools), or used deliberately to bypass monitoring.

Accidental or deliberate damage

Accidental: fire/smoke damage, water/flood damage from burst pipes, power outages corrupting unsaved data.

Deliberate: insider sabotage during disputes, terrorism/vandalism, tampering with backup systems or power sources.

Weak cyber security & unsafe practices

Unlocked terminals Unattended storage devices Poor visitor security Unsafe website use No 2FA / weak passwords

Each of these creates a vulnerability an attacker can exploit, or lets data be exposed by accident.

Accidental loss or disclosure — key causes

  • Human error — e.g. emailing the wrong recipient.
  • Lack of training — staff unaware of phishing tactics.
  • Procedures not followed — unencrypted data shared.
  • Poor monitoring — threats not caught early.
  • Weak security culture — no incentive to report issues.
Case study

Disgruntled employee, logistics company

A disgruntled employee installs unauthorised keystroke-logging software that sends data to a personal email. The company later suffers a breach involving customer delivery data — a direct line from unauthorised software → data theft → reputational and financial damage.

4 marks

Give two examples of internal cyber security threats caused by employees, and explain how each one can impact an organisation.

Show model answer
Theft of data — an employee deliberately copies/steals sensitive files, risking a data breach and legal action. Use of unauthorised software — installing software without approval can introduce malware or compatibility issues, causing system failures or new vulnerabilities.
6 marks

An organisation suffers a breach after an employee visits an untrustworthy website and downloads malicious software. Discuss the internal weaknesses that contributed and how to prevent recurrence.

Show model answer
Likely a mix of weak controls and human error: no effective web filtering/endpoint protection, poor training so the employee didn't recognise the risk, a weak security culture that doesn't encourage safe practice or reporting, and insufficient monitoring to catch it early. Prevention: strict web filtering and malware detection, regular awareness training, clear acceptable-use policies, and active network monitoring.

LESSON 2.2 – 2.3

Malware

External threats come from outside the organisation. This is the first of four external-threat categories — malicious software.

Viruses — 9 types to know

TypeWhat it does
Boot sectorInfects the startup sector of storage
Web scriptInjected into webpages
MacroHidden inside documents/spreadsheets
WormSelf-replicates across systems
RootkitHides malware activity from the OS
Trojan horseDisguised as legitimate software
Browser hijackerRedirects web traffic
Polymorphic virusChanges its own code to avoid detection
TSR residentStays resident in memory, slows performance, opens weaknesses

Spyware

Covert software that steals data or monitors activity.

  • Keyloggers — record keystrokes to steal credentials
  • System monitors — track all user behaviour
  • Stalkerware / mobile trackers — GPS, calls, messages
  • Web beacons / cookies — advertising & tracking

Adware

  • Legitimate adware — shows ads in exchange for free use
  • PUPs (Potentially Unwanted Programs) — installed without real consent
  • Abusive/deceptive adware — can be illegal or intrusive

Ransomware

  • Encryptors — lock files until a ransom is paid
  • Lockers — block access to the whole system
  • Scareware — fake threats to force payment
  • Leakware/doxware — threatens to publish stolen data

Bots

Programs that automate tasks; malicious bots form botnets used for spam, DoS attacks, and crypto-mining. Remote-controlled by attackers, they can install further malware.

Case study

Colonial Pipeline, 2021

A ransomware attack shut down Colonial Pipeline in the US, disrupting fuel supply across the East Coast. Attackers demanded a large cryptocurrency ransom — a real example of how ransomware can hit critical national infrastructure, not just single businesses.

4 marks

Identify two types of vulnerabilities related to software and explain how they can be exploited by attackers.

Show model answer
Operating System vulnerabilities — attackers can exploit unpatched OS flaws to gain unauthorised access. Software bugs — bugs in applications can allow malware injection or data leaks.

LESSON 2.4 – 2.5

Hacking & Sabotage

Two more external threat categories: unauthorised access to systems, and deliberate destruction or disruption of them.

Hacking

Unauthorised access to systems or data, carried out by:

  • Individuals (black-hat hackers)
  • Companies (corporate espionage)
  • Governments (cyberwarfare)

Types: Denial of Service (DoS/DDoS) overwhelms a system; browser hijacking redirects traffic; data theft/tampering hits the back-end (databases) or front-end (web forms).

Sabotage

Intentional destruction or disruption of digital or physical systems.

  • Data poisoning — corrupting training data/analytics
  • Data tampering — unauthorised alteration of data
  • Data destruction — deleting or altering records
  • Fake content — deepfakes, forged AI-generated documents
  • Hijacking devices — machinery, IoT, vehicles, infrastructure

Case study

Government agency phishing attack

Hackers deploy malware through a phishing campaign and gain access to sensitive national data. Likely chain: phishing email → malware installed → backdoor/credential logging → lateral access. Contributing weaknesses: poor email filtering, no MFA, weak network segmentation, untrained staff. Fix: staff training, advanced email filtering & anti-malware tools, stronger access controls.

4 marks

Define social engineering and give two examples of how it can be used to gain access to secure information.

Show model answer
Social engineering is the use of deception to manipulate individuals into revealing confidential or personal information. Examples: phishing emails that appear to come from a trusted source, tricking users into entering login details; pretexting, where an attacker pretends to be an IT technician to gain access to systems or passwords.

LESSON 2.8

Impact of a Credible Threat

Five categories of loss examiners expect you to recognise and connect to a scenario: operational, financial (organisational and legal), reputational, and intellectual property.

Operational loss

  • Manufacturing output — production systems halted by malware or network failure
  • Service availability — websites, apps or customer services go down
  • Data availability — ransomware locks access to vital records/databases

Result: disrupted business continuity and reduced performance.

Organisational financial impact

  • Cash flow problems — no income while services are halted
  • Loss of profits — missed sales, cancelled orders
  • Increased insurance premiums — seen as higher risk

Financial compensation costs

  • Refunds for lost goods/failed services
  • Compensation for leaked/misused personal data
  • Remedial costs — credit monitoring, ID protection, IT audits
  • Contractual fines for failing to deliver services
  • Discounts/write-offs to repair customer relationships

Legal financial loss

  • Fines for breaching data protection law (e.g. GDPR)
  • Penalty payments stated in contracts
  • Investigation costs following a breach/outage
  • Legal representation fees

Reputational loss

  • Loss of customer trust
  • Negative reviews (Google, Trustpilot, social media)
  • Poor scores on cyber-security watchdog sites

Trust is hard to gain, easy to lose — this is often the longest-lasting cost of an attack.

Intellectual property (IP) loss

IP is the valuable data, knowledge or strategy a business owns. Losses include new product designs, development/testing data, organisational strategy, and trade secrets — often hard to detect until the damage is already done.

Case study

British Airways, 2018

Hackers stole personal and payment data belonging to 400,000 customers. BA was fined £20m under GDPR, faced furious customers seeking compensation, and had to rebuild trust while investing in new security — operational, financial, legal and reputational loss all in one incident.

6 marks

An organisation faces a credible threat from a known hacking group targeting its customer database. Discuss the potential consequences for its operations, finances, and reputation.

Show model answer
Hacking could cause operational disruption if systems are taken offline to stop further breaches. There may be financial losses from emergency IT costs and potential legal fines under data protection law. If customer data leaks, the company faces reputational damage — losing client trust and future business. Long term, this can mean legal liability and lasting damage to brand credibility; proactive incident response and communication plans limit the impact.

LESSON 2.9

Evolving Threat Landscape

Three organisations examiners expect you to be able to name, describe, and compare.

NCSC — UK

National Cyber Security Centre

The UK's authority on cyber security, part of GCHQ. Works with businesses, education, government and individuals.

Provides: weekly threat reports and technical briefings, malware analysis, advisories on phishing/ransomware campaigns, and practical prevention/recovery guidance.

NIST — USA

National Institute of Standards and Technology

A US agency that sets global cyber security standards and maintains a comprehensive Cybersecurity Framework (CSF), focused on policy, research and guidelines.

Provides: advisories/bulletins on emerging threats, risk management frameworks, incident detection/response guidance, and research publications.

OWASP — global, non-profit

Open Web Application Security Project

Focused on improving software and web application security; maintains community-driven tools and resources for developers, educators and businesses.

OWASP Top 10 — the regularly updated list of the most serious web risks:

Broken access control Cryptographic failures Injection (e.g. SQL) Insecure design Security misconfiguration Vulnerable components Auth failures Software/data integrity failures Logging & monitoring failures Server-side request forgery
Case study

SolarWinds hack

Hackers injected malware into updates of globally-used SolarWinds software, compromising US government agencies and major corporations. The attack was detected by FireEye and reported to NCSC, NIST and other global bodies — thousands of systems were silently compromised before discovery, showing why international cooperation between these organisations matters.

8 marks

An IT manager wants to strengthen the company's cyber security policy, considering guidance from NIST, NCSC and OWASP.

Show model answer
NIST provides widely-adopted frameworks like the Cybersecurity Framework (CSF), helping organisations assess and improve their security posture. NCSC gives UK-focused threat intelligence, incident-handling guidance and best practice for public and private sectors. OWASP supports secure software development through open-source resources like the OWASP Top Ten, highlighting common web vulnerabilities. Using all three, the manager can build a well-rounded policy covering technical, procedural and development risks.

LESSON 2.10 – 2.11

System Vulnerabilities

A vulnerability is a weakness or flaw — in hardware, software, processes or user behaviour — that could be exploited to gain unauthorised access or cause harm.

Network vulnerabilities

Open or misconfigured firewall ports are a major risk. Ports can be well-known, registered, or dynamic/private (temporary).

PortProtocolUsed for
21FTPUploading files to a web server
25SMTPSending & receiving email
80HTTPConnecting to a web server to view pages
443HTTPSSecure web traffic

Other risks: open unprotected ports, spoofing (pretending to be a trusted source), and DoS attacks that overwhelm the system.

External storage devices

USB/flash drives, SD cards, external hard drives, and SSDs are portable — meaning they can be stolen or deliberately used to insert malware. Organisations should have clear policies on their use, or require password protection, encryption and GPS tracking (with remote-wipe for lost devices).

Organisational vulnerabilities

Permissions/privileges — correct access level per role (read/write/execute/modify), correct account type (user/admin/system/guest), and control over which databases/services/devices a user can reach.

Password policy — length rules, character restrictions, no shared/reused passwords, no personal info, minimum password age, no password sharing.

Password management — password managers (device-based, synced, or cloud-based) create and store strong complex passwords; policies should also cover locking inactive accounts, onboarding/offboarding, and disabling autofill.

Software vulnerabilities

Software from untrustworthy sources or illegal downloads often hides malware or backdoors. Even trusted software can have zero-day exploits — flaws attackers use before a patch exists. Internet-facing systems without SQL injection protection are frequent targets. Regular updates and security scans are essential.

Operating systems

Unsupported OSes and missing updates are major risks. Misconfigured security settings — in either GUI or CLI — can leave systems exposed. Keeping updates and patches applied is crucial.

Mobile devices & OEM dependency

Mobile devices depend on their Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM) for updates. If an OEM delays or stops support, the device stays vulnerable — often without the user realising their device is unprotected.

Physical vulnerabilities

Devices holding sensitive data can be stolen where physical security is weak. Unrestricted areas, unlocked offices, or unauthorised USB use lead to data theft or loss. Lockable cabinets, security cameras and staff ID access reduce the risk.

Human processes & behaviour

People are often the weakest link — accidental leaks, shared credentials, or poor onboarding/offboarding all expose systems. Training, well-defined procedures, and regular audits help reduce human-related vulnerabilities.

Cloud & IoT vulnerabilities

Cloud computing depends on external providers — risks include default settings, third-party access, and data travelling over the internet. IoT devices often lack encryption and use default passwords; deployed by non-IT staff, they can introduce serious flaws, including eavesdropping.

Case study

UK healthcare breach, 2023

A UK healthcare provider suffered a breach after: running an unpatched operating system, staff sharing admin passwords, and using a connected IoT blood-pressure monitor with no encryption. Data of 20,000 patients was compromised — three separate vulnerability types combining into one incident.

6 marks

An organisation is concerned about vulnerabilities in its network and mobile devices provided by OEMs. Discuss the types of vulnerabilities and suggest ways to mitigate the risks.

Show model answer
Network vulnerabilities include weak permissions/privileges (attackers exploit these for higher access) and poorly configured devices allowing unauthorised entry or interception. OEM mobile devices can carry built-in vulnerabilities from manufacturer defaults or delayed security updates, risking data theft or malware. Mitigation: enforce strict user permissions, regularly update software/firmware, use firewalls and intrusion detection systems, and apply device-management policies with timely patching for mobiles.

LESSON 2.12

Attack Vectors

An attack vector is the route or method a cybercriminal uses to gain access to a computer or network — the "open window" a burglar would look for, translated into digital terms.

Wireless attack vectors

  • Wi-Fi (802.11) — susceptible to man-in-the-middle attacks if unencrypted/open; weak WPA/WEP passwords are easily cracked
  • Bluetooth — vulnerable to "bluesnarfing" or "bluejacking"; exploitable if left in discoverable mode
  • Cellular & satellite — spoofing or eavesdropping on unencrypted comms; interception possible with special hardware
  • Infrared — rare now, but older devices' line-of-sight transmission could be intercepted
  • NFC & RFID — short range but vulnerable to cloning, skimming, or relay attacks (access cards, contactless payment)

Internet connection vectors

Connection types: copper cable (older, vulnerable to signal tapping), optical fibre (more secure, but still at risk with physical access), Wi-Fi/5G (packet sniffing, fake access points, rogue cell towers).

Devices: modem/router with default credentials or outdated firmware is a prime target; 5G routers can be SIM-swapped or spoofed; wireless routers risk open ports and remote-access flaws.

Internal network access devices

Router — distributes internet access; attackable via open admin ports, DNS hijacking, firmware flaws.

Switch — directs internal traffic; attackers may use MAC flooding to intercept it.

Wireless Access Point (WAP) — if unsecured, gives attackers a foothold on the internal network to launch further attacks.

Case study

Target data breach, 2013

Hackers accessed Target's internal network through an HVAC subcontractor's internet connection, then moved laterally inside the network — 40 million customer credit card records were stolen. Caused by poor network segmentation, weak third-party router access, and no internal traffic monitoring.

6 marks

Discuss how attack vectors involving wireless, internet connections, and internal devices can lead to security incidents, and suggest mitigation strategies for each.

Show model answer
Wireless networks can be exploited via weak encryption or default passwords, letting attackers eavesdrop or connect. Internet connections expose systems to phishing or malware through browsing/email. Internal devices (workstations, IoT) can be compromised if unsecured, giving attackers a foothold inside. Mitigation: strong Wi-Fi encryption (WPA3), regular patching of all devices, firewalls and intrusion detection, and strict access controls with network segmentation.
